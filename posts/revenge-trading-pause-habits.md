Every trader knows the feeling. A decision goes badly, your chest tightens, and a voice says you can fix this right now. The next decision comes quickly and it is not really a decision at all. It is a reaction.

That is revenge trading: acting to get back at a loss instead of following your plan. This post is about why it happens and about the habits that put a pause between the loss and your next move. It is not about what to trade or how. It is about the moment in between.

## Why it happens

Revenge trading is not a sign that you are undisciplined. It is a very human response to something that stings. A loss can feel like a threat, or like an insult, and the urge to undo it is strong.

A few things tend to make the urge stronger:

- **Being tired.** The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute notes that sleep deficiency can make it harder to make decisions, control emotions and behavior, and cope with change.
- **Stress that has been building.** Stress is the body's response to an external pressure, and the National Institute of Mental Health points out that it can affect both the mind and the body, with symptoms such as tension, headaches and loss of sleep.
- **No plan for the moment.** If you have not decided in advance what you will do after a rough decision, you will decide in the heat of it.

Notice that none of these is about skill. They are about state and preparation, and both can be improved with habits.

## Learn your early signals

You cannot pause if you do not notice the moment. Most people have physical and mental signals that show up before they act on impulse. Common ones include:

- A faster heartbeat or tight shoulders.
- Thoughts like "I have to make this back" or "I'll just do one more".
- Leaning toward the screen and sitting still.
- Skipping the steps of your usual routine.
- Feeling unusually certain.

Spend a week noticing yours, and write them down in your journal. The more specific you are, the easier they are to catch. A signal you can name is a signal you can act on.

## Decide your pause before you need it

The most reliable way to create a pause is to decide it in advance, when you are calm. Write it into your plan so that in the moment there is nothing to negotiate.

Here are some pause habits that many people find realistic. Pick one or two.

**Stand up.** The simplest rule is that after a decision that stings, you stand up and leave the desk before you do anything else. Walk to a window, refill a glass of water, step outside.

**Set a timer.** Agree with yourself on a fixed amount of time, say ten minutes, before the next decision. The exact number matters less than the fact that it is fixed and decided beforehand.

**Breathe slowly.** A few slow breaths will not solve anything, but they interrupt the momentum of the moment. The NIMH lists deep breathing and mindfulness exercises among the things people can try when they feel overwhelmed.

**Write one sentence.** Before you do anything else, write one line in your journal: "I just felt ___ because ___." Naming a feeling in writing slows you down, and it gives you something to look back on.

**Tell someone.** If you have an accountability partner, a quick message can be enough. You do not have to share numbers. "Rough moment, taking ten" is plenty.

## Make the pause easy

A pause that depends on willpower will fail when you are most upset. Reduce the friction:

- Put the rule where you will see it, on a sticky note at your desk or at the top of your plan.
- Keep the first step tiny. "Stand up" is easier to do than "reflect on my psychology".
- Decide what happens at the end of the pause. It might be to return to your plan, or to finish for the day. Having an ending in mind keeps the pause from turning into avoidance.
- Agree with yourself that a pause is always allowed, whatever happened.

## Build a stronger base

Pauses help in the moment, but a steady base makes the moment less likely to arrive. The same ordinary habits that appear in most general well-being advice are relevant here.

Sleep matters. The NHLBI recommends adults get about seven to eight hours a day and suggests keeping a regular sleep and wake time. Regular meals, movement and time away from the screen also help. The NIMH lists exercise, healthy regular meals, a sleep routine and limiting excess caffeine among its tips for handling stress.

A review habit helps too. At the end of the day, take a few minutes to look back on how you handled the hard moments. Not to punish yourself, but to see what you did, and whether your pause happened. If it did not, ask what would have made it easier.

## Be kind to yourself afterward

If you act on the urge anyway, and most people do at some point, the next step is not self-criticism. It is the same as before: stand up, breathe, write one sentence about what happened, and adjust your pause for next time. Harsh self-criticism rarely helps you do better next time.

If losses leave you feeling low or anxious for more than a short while, or you find it hard to cope, it is worth talking to a doctor or mental health professional. If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 in the U.S. to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

It also helps to remember that trading carries real risk, and losses are part of it. A habit like a pause is about how you respond, not about avoiding that risk.
