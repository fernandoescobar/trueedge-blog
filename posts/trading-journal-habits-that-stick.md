Ask a room of traders whether they should keep a journal and nearly everyone says yes. Ask how many still do, three months after starting, and the room goes quiet.

The usual reason is not laziness. It is that the journal was too big. A page of notes per entry, screenshots, a long list of fields to fill in: it feels thorough on day one and heavy by day ten. Good trading journal habits start with the opposite idea. Make the journal so small that skipping it feels sillier than doing it.

This post covers how to keep a trading journal that you will still be using next spring.

## Why a journal at all

A journal gives you a record of what you actually did and how you felt while you did it. Memory is a poor substitute. We tend to remember the dramatic moments and forget the quiet patterns, and we smooth over decisions we are not proud of.

Writing things down is also a general coping habit. The National Institute of Mental Health lists keeping a journal among the activities people can try when they feel overwhelmed, alongside exercise, regular meals and a steady sleep routine. A journal will not fix stress, but it gives your thoughts somewhere to land other than your next decision.

For traders there is a further benefit. A journal that records your process, and not only your results, lets you look back and notice patterns in your own behavior over weeks and months.

## The three-line rule

Here is a format that is hard to quit. At the end of your day, write three lines:

1. **What I did.** One line on how your day went, in terms of your process. Did you follow your plan for the day? Did you take your breaks?
2. **How I felt.** One line on your state. Calm, rushed, bored, anxious, overconfident. Be honest and be specific.
3. **One thing for tomorrow.** A single small adjustment, or nothing at all.

That is the whole thing. It takes two or three minutes. Most days you will want to write more, and that is fine, but the minimum is three lines.

Notice what is missing: there are no market observations and no commentary about what you think will happen next. Those belong, if anywhere, in a different document. A journal about your behavior stays useful long after any particular day's conditions are forgotten.

## Write about decisions and feelings, not only numbers

A common mistake is turning the journal into a spreadsheet. Numbers have their place, but they cannot tell you why you made a choice. The "why" is where the learning is.

When you write, try prompts like these:

- What was I thinking just before I made my biggest decision today?
- When did I feel the urge to rush, and what did I do about it?
- Did I take a break when I planned to?
- What was my energy like this morning compared with this afternoon?

You do not need to answer all of them. Pick one. Over time you may notice that the same feelings show up before the same kinds of decisions. That is the sort of pattern a journal helps you see.

## Make it a habit, not a project

Many people find that small actions tied to something they already do each day are easier to repeat. Here are practical ways to apply that.

**Pick a fixed time and a fixed place.** After you close your platform for the day, or right after dinner, or with your evening tea. The cue should be something that already happens every day.

**Use the same notebook or file.** Do not spend energy deciding where to write. Keep the notebook open on your desk, or pin a single note on your phone.

**Lower the bar on bad days.** If you are tired or frustrated, write one line. "Rough day, tired, will try again tomorrow" counts. A journal that records your hard days is more useful than one that only records your good ones.

**Do not edit or judge.** Nobody is going to grade your journal. Spelling, grammar and tidy handwriting do not matter. If you catch yourself writing for an imaginary audience, stop and write what is true.

## Do a short review, but not too often

A journal you never read is a diary. A journal you read every day becomes a source of anxiety. A middle path works for many people: once a week, spend ten or fifteen minutes reading the last seven days of entries.

While you read, look for three things:

- Feelings or situations that keep reappearing.
- Days when you stuck to your routine, and days when you did not.
- Small changes you made that seemed to help your state, such as an earlier bedtime or a walk after lunch.

Write one sentence at the end of the review about what you noticed. Keep it about patterns in your own behavior, and be cautious about drawing conclusions. Two things happening together does not mean one caused the other.

## When you fall behind

You will. The key is how you restart. Do not try to catch up by writing five days of entries from memory. Skip them, write today's three lines and carry on. A gap in the journal is a fact, not a failure.

If you have missed more than a week, it is a sign that the format is too heavy or the time of day is wrong. Shrink the format or change the cue and try again.

## A note on privacy and well-being

Your journal is yours. Decide in advance whether you will ever share any of it, and with whom. Some people find it helpful to share one line a week with a trusted friend or mentor. Others keep everything private. Both are fine.

If your journal starts to reveal that you feel persistently low, anxious or unable to cope, that is worth taking seriously. A journal is not a replacement for professional support. If you are struggling, talking to a doctor or a mental health professional is a good next step. If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text 988 in the U.S. to reach the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.
