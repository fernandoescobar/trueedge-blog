Sleep is the least glamorous part of a trader's process and probably the most underrated. It does not feel like a skill, and it rarely gets a place on a checklist. Yet almost everything you do at your desk, from concentrating to keeping your composure, rests on it.

This post looks at what reputable health sources say about sleep and decision-making, and then builds a simple evening routine around it. If you have ever wondered about sleep for traders, here is the short version: it is a habit like any other, and it can be improved in small steps.

## What the research says

The U.S. National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI), part of the National Institutes of Health, says that getting enough quality sleep at the right times can help protect your mental health, physical health, quality of life and safety.

On the thinking side, the NHLBI describes sleep deficiency as something that can cause problems with learning, focusing and reacting. It adds that you may have trouble making decisions, solving problems, remembering things, managing your emotions and behavior, and coping with change. You may also take longer to finish tasks, react more slowly and make more mistakes.

The same page says that while you sleep, your brain is getting ready for the next day, forming new pathways that help you learn and remember. It also notes that studies show a good night's sleep improves learning and problem-solving skills, and helps you pay attention, make decisions and be creative.

None of this is specific to trading, and it does not say anything about results. But it describes, quite closely, the mental qualities that a long day of screen-based decisions asks for.

## How much is enough

For adults, the NHLBI cites recommendations from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine of seven to eight hours a day. Needs vary from person to person, so treat that as a general guide and not a target to be anxious about.

The NHLBI also explains that regularly losing sleep adds up to a sleep debt. If you lose two hours a night, that is fourteen hours after a week. Naps can give a short-term boost in alertness, but they do not supply all the benefits of night-time sleep, so you cannot fully make up for lost sleep.

Sleeping much longer on your days off can be a sign you are not getting enough during the week. It can also upset your body's sleep-wake rhythm.

## Why traders are at risk

Some jobs make sleep harder, and trading can be one of them. Early starts, an evening of reading and review, a late finish after a stressful day, and a screen-heavy routine all work against a steady schedule. If you trade a market that opens at an unusual hour for where you live, the problem is worse.

The NHLBI lists people with limited time for sleep, people whose schedules conflict with their body clocks, and people dealing with stress or anxiety among those more likely to be sleep deficient. If that sounds familiar, you are in good company, and you can do something about it.

## An evening routine that protects sleep

You do not need an elaborate ritual. The NHLBI's healthy sleep habits give a practical starting point. Here is how to turn them into an evening routine.

**1. Decide your lights-out time first.** Work backward from your wake-up time. The NHLBI suggests going to bed and waking up at the same time every day, and keeping the difference between weeknights and weekends to no more than about an hour.

**2. Set a caffeine cutoff.** The NHLBI says the effects of caffeine can last up to eight hours, so a cup of coffee in the late afternoon can make it hard to fall asleep. Pick a time after which you switch to water or decaf.

**3. Finish work at a set time.** Close your platform, then close the day with a short review. Three lines in a journal are enough: what happened, how you felt, one thing for tomorrow. The National Institute of Mental Health lists keeping a journal among its suggestions for coping when you feel overwhelmed.

**4. Eat earlier and lighter.** The NHLBI advises avoiding heavy or large meals within a few hours of bedtime, and avoiding alcoholic drinks before bed. A light snack is fine.

**5. Use the last hour for quiet.** The NHLBI recommends quiet time in the hour before bed, avoiding intense exercise and bright artificial light, such as from a TV or computer screen, because light can signal the brain that it is time to be awake. A practical version: charge your phone across the room, read a few pages of a book and dim the lights.

**6. Keep the room right.** The NHLBI suggests a bedroom that is quiet, cool and dark.

## Make it stick

Start with one change. The easiest for most people is a fixed lights-out time or a caffeine cutoff. Do that for two weeks, then add another.

Tie each habit to something you already do. Brushing your teeth can be the cue to put your phone away. Closing your trading platform can be the cue to write your three lines.

Track it lightly. A simple tick on a calendar or in a habit tracker when you meet your lights-out time is enough. Look at the pattern each week, not each night. One bad night does not undo anything.

## On the days you sleep badly

It will happen. On those days, go easier on yourself and on your plan. Keep your routine short, take your breaks, and be honest in your journal about how tired you felt. Tiredness is useful information. Remember what the NHLBI says about sleep deficiency and decision-making, and treat your own decisions that day with a little extra care.

If you regularly struggle to sleep, feel sleepy during the day despite spending enough time in bed, or sleep more than eight hours and still do not feel rested, the NHLBI suggests talking with your doctor, since there may be a sleep disorder or other health problem. A general article cannot diagnose anything, and a doctor can.
